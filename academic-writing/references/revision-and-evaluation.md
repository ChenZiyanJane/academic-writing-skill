# Revision, Editing, and Paper Evaluation

## Revision comes before editing

The book distinguishes higher-level revision from lower-level editing. Preserve that order.

### Revision
Focus on:
- content;
- argument;
- evidence;
- purpose;
- organization;
- section function.

### Editing
Focus on:
- grammar;
- spelling;
- punctuation;
- sentence structure;
- word choice;
- consistency;
- documentation mechanics.

Do not spend most of the effort polishing sentences that may need to be deleted or reorganized.

## Content revision checklist

Ask:
- Is the central argument or thesis clearly presented?
- Is the evidence convincing and sufficient for the claims?
- Is there irrelevant or unnecessary information?
- Is the reasoning developed logically throughout?
- Is the writer's point of view consistent?
- Do supporting details actually support the central claim?
- Does the introduction create a useful entry into the research problem?
- Is the analysis clear and sufficiently objective for the genre?
- Does the conclusion follow logically from the paper?

## Organization revision checklist

Ask:
- Are all relevant major parts present?
- Is the overall arrangement logical?
- Does the introduction provide the information needed to understand the study?
- Does the methods section explain procedures adequately?
- Does the results section present findings clearly and systematically?
- Does the discussion interpret findings rather than merely repeat them?
- Does the conclusion close the paper effectively?

At paragraph level, also check:
- one controlling point per paragraph where appropriate;
- logical order of supporting sentences;
- explicit relation to the larger section;
- transitions that express real relationships rather than decorative connectors.

## Editing checklist

Check:
- punctuation;
- spelling;
- verb tense consistency;
- subject-verb and pronoun agreement;
- sentence completeness and grammaticality;
- sentence-structure variety without sacrificing clarity;
- precise and appropriate word choice;
- proper transitions;
- consistent academic register;
- consistent documentation style;
- completeness of citations and reference entries.

## Supplementary parts

A complete thesis or research paper may also require:
- cover/title page;
- table of contents;
- acknowledgements;
- lists of tables and figures;
- list of abbreviations;
- appendices.

Treat these as institution-dependent. Do not assume every assignment needs them.

## Evaluating a complete paper

Unit 9 converts the earlier teaching points into a review framework. Apply it section by section.

### Title
- informative;
- concise;
- revealing of the research area or problem.

### Abstract
Check for the relevant combination of:
- research topic/area;
- purpose;
- background/problem;
- methods;
- results/findings;
- conclusions/implications/recommendations.

### Introduction
Check whether it:
- introduces the topic;
- reviews enough prior research;
- focuses on the issue;
- states the purpose or present research;
- indicates the paper structure when useful.

### Literature review
Check whether it:
- covers the relevant research area;
- synthesizes and evaluates scholarship;
- is organized by a clear principle;
- establishes a theoretical or empirical foundation;
- supports the research question.

### Methods
Check:
- what/instrument;
- who/subjects or data source;
- how/procedures and data collection.

### Results
Check:
- data presentation;
- data description;
- clarity of tables/figures if used;
- alignment with the research questions.

### Discussion
Check whether it:
- explains findings;
- supplies meaning rather than only statistics;
- links findings to the research purpose and prior work;
- identifies implications or limitations.

### Conclusion
Check whether it:
- restates the central outcome;
- summarizes the research design/results/interpretation as appropriate;
- proposes future research or recommendations only when justified.

## Sample-paper lessons

The book's sample papers are used as diagnostic material rather than perfect models. General lessons to preserve:
- a complete-looking outline can still be logically weak;
- an introduction can contain background yet fail to define the research problem;
- a literature review can be long yet poorly synthesized;
- classification schemes need a consistent basis;
- sections must match their labels and rhetorical functions;
- quotations and references must be integrated and documented consistently;
- conclusions should synthesize rather than introduce unrelated new material.

## Priority system for feedback

When giving whole-paper feedback, rank issues:

**Priority 1: research validity**
- unclear question;
- unsupported claim;
- method-question mismatch;
- missing evidence.

**Priority 2: paper architecture**
- missing section function;
- weak literature-review logic;
- poor sequence;
- results/discussion confusion.

**Priority 3: paragraph reasoning and source use**
- weak topic focus;
- evidence not interpreted;
- citation or paraphrase problems.

**Priority 4: language and mechanics**
- grammar;
- diction;
- punctuation;
- formatting.

Always help the user fix Priority 1 and 2 issues before spending most of the response on Priority 4.

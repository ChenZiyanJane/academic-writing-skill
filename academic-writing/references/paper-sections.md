# Drafting and Major Paper Sections

## Purpose of drafting

Drafting turns research notes and an outline into a testable argument. It is a stage for discovering whether the writer's ideas actually work together.

A draft should help the writer:
- clarify purpose;
- identify the central argument;
- test the organization;
- integrate evidence;
- discover missing information;
- expose contradictions, repetition, or gaps.

## General drafting principles

- Work from an outline, but revise the outline when evidence demands it.
- Keep terminology, tone, documentation, and mechanics consistent.
- Use specialized vocabulary only when it helps precision.
- Attribute other writers' ideas.
- Present facts, details, statistics, and quotations accurately.
- Avoid claims broader than the evidence supports.
- Make the parts of the argument connect logically.
- Show the writer's own reasoning rather than stitching sources together.

## Abstract

The abstract is a compact representation of the paper and often a reader's first source of information.

Check whether it answers, as applicable:
1. What is the research area or topic?
2. What is the research purpose?
3. What background or problem makes the study necessary?
4. What method was used?
5. What were the main results or findings?
6. What conclusions, implications, or recommendations follow?

Do not turn the abstract into a long introduction. Include only the information needed to represent the study accurately.

## Introduction

Common functions:
- introduce the topic;
- establish background;
- review enough prior research to locate the issue;
- narrow to the problem or gap;
- state purpose or research questions;
- indicate paper structure when useful.

The introduction should move from context to the specific research problem. Background that never contributes to that movement should be cut or relocated.

## Methods

The methods section explains how the study was carried out.

At minimum, answer:
- **What?** instruments, materials, tests, questionnaires, corpora, texts, or other data sources;
- **Who?** participants, subjects, sample, or unit of analysis;
- **How?** procedures, data collection, coding, analysis, and relevant conditions.

Provide enough detail to judge the fit between method and research question.

## Results

The results section primarily:
- presents findings;
- describes what the data show.

Use tables, charts, figures, or examples when they improve comprehension. Do not repeat every number in prose. Direct the reader to the pattern that matters.

Prefer verbs such as indicate, show, reveal, report, or describe when appropriate. Keep interpretation limited if the paper separates results from discussion.

## Discussion

The discussion explains what the findings mean.

It may:
- interpret patterns;
- relate findings to the research purpose;
- compare findings with previous studies;
- explain surprising or contradictory results;
- assess significance;
- acknowledge strengths and limitations;
- discuss what can and cannot be concluded.

A discussion should not simply restate the results in different words.

## Conclusion

A conclusion commonly includes:
- restatement of the central outcome or answer;
- concise synthesis of major findings;
- implications;
- limitations;
- future research or recommendations where justified.

The conclusion should close the paper's argument. Do not introduce a major new line of evidence at the end.

## Integrating evidence into paragraphs

A strong research paragraph often contains:
1. a clear point or claim;
2. evidence from data or sources;
3. explanation of why that evidence matters;
4. qualification or limitation when needed;
5. a link to the larger research question.

Do not allow quotations or data to sit without interpretation.

## Section-function test

When reviewing a draft, ask:
- What function is this section supposed to perform?
- What information is necessary for that function?
- What information belongs elsewhere?
- What is missing?
- Is the section doing its job before sentence-level polishing begins?

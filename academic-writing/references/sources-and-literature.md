# Sources, Library Research, and Literature Review

## Preliminary source research

Use secondary research to understand what is already known before designing a full study. The writer should learn to locate, inspect, evaluate, and document sources rather than collect material indiscriminately.

## Source categories

Typical library or scholarly sources include:
- reference works;
- books and monographs;
- edited collections;
- scholarly journals;
- theses or dissertations;
- conference papers or proceedings;
- credible web sources;
- primary texts or documents when the research design requires them.

Treat search platforms and interfaces as replaceable tools. Preserve the search logic, not the book's old screenshots.

## Evaluating sources

Check at least:

### Relevance
Does the source directly help answer the research question or clarify a necessary concept?

### Objectivity / evidential fit
Is the source presenting evidence or an argument in a way suitable for the user's purpose? Bias does not automatically disqualify a source, but its standpoint must be understood.

### Credibility
Consider author expertise, publisher or venue, evidence base, review process, methods, and scholarly reception where available.

### Currency and temporal fit
Use current sources when the topic requires current evidence. Older sources may remain essential for theory, history, or classic debates.

## Efficient source inspection

Before reading a book or long work in full, inspect:
- title and publication information;
- author credentials when relevant;
- table of contents;
- introduction/preface;
- index;
- chapter headings;
- references or bibliography;
- sections directly tied to the research question.

Use skimming and scanning to decide whether deeper reading is justified.

## Working bibliography and source notes

Maintain a working bibliography from the beginning. For each source, capture enough information to identify it later and to avoid accidental plagiarism.

Source notes should distinguish:
- exact quotation;
- paraphrase;
- summary;
- writer's own reaction or interpretation.

Never allow copied wording to lose its source label in notes.

## What a literature review must do

A literature review is a critical account of relevant published knowledge, not a sequence of article summaries.

Require the review to:
1. establish the theoretical or empirical foundation of the study;
2. identify what has already been discovered, argued, or tested;
3. reveal gaps, conflicts, limitations, or unresolved questions;
4. position the present research in relation to prior work;
5. help formulate or justify the research question.

## Organizing a literature review

### Chronological organization
Use when development over time matters. Group sources by meaningful periods and explain what changed, persisted, or emerged in each period.

Do not merely sort by publication year. The chronology needs a guiding concept.

### Thematic organization
Use when the literature contains recurring concepts, problems, theories, methods, or findings. Group studies under themes and synthesize within each theme.

The theme label should express an analytical category, not just a source name.

### Other valid organizing principles
When appropriate, organize by:
- theory;
- method;
- population;
- variable;
- debate or position;
- geographic context;
- type of evidence.

State the organizing principle explicitly.

## Synthesis and evaluation

Within a paragraph or subsection:
1. state the guiding point;
2. bring together multiple relevant sources;
3. compare agreements, differences, methods, or limitations;
4. evaluate what the body of work establishes;
5. connect that evaluation to the present study.

Avoid the pattern: Author A says X. Author B says Y. Author C says Z. Instead, synthesize around the research issue.

## Gap discipline

Do not declare a "research gap" merely because a topic is interesting or no identical title was found. A defensible gap may involve:
- an unresolved contradiction;
- an understudied population/context;
- a methodological limitation;
- insufficient evidence;
- an untested relationship;
- an outdated evidence base;
- a theoretical issue not adequately explained.

The gap must follow from the reviewed literature.

## Documentation

Use a consistent citation system required by the user's institution or publication. The source book illustrates MLA and APA conventions, but exact formatting has changed over time.

If current style precision matters, verify current rules instead of copying the book's 2013 examples.

# Field Research and Basic Data Handling

## Role of field research

Use field/primary research when the project needs firsthand evidence about what people do, think, experience, produce, or say. The book presents three common methods: observation, questionnaire, and interview.

Do not choose a method because it is familiar. Choose it because it can answer the research question.

## Observation

### What observation is for
Observation records behavior, events, interactions, performance, or other phenomena as they occur or soon afterward.

### Planning questions
Before observing, define:
- **why** the observation is needed;
- **what** exactly will be observed;
- **how** observations will be recorded;
- **who/what** will be observed;
- **where and when** observation will occur;
- **what equipment** or recording form is needed.

### Recording methods
Possible formats include:
- anecdotal records;
- checklists;
- rating scales;
- structured field notes;
- audio/video only when appropriate and permitted.

### Observation quality
Aim to be:
- unobtrusive when possible;
- attentive;
- accurate;
- systematic;
- detailed enough to preserve context;
- prompt in recording information.

Separate what was observed from the observer's interpretation.

## Questionnaire

## Purpose
A questionnaire collects responses from a defined target population using a written set of questions.

## Basic design workflow

1. Define the research objective.
2. Define the target population.
3. Decide what information is needed.
4. Write questions tied directly to that information need.
5. Order questions logically.
6. Pilot or seek feedback when possible.
7. Administer consistently.
8. Check responses before analysis.
9. Analyze according to the question types and research purpose.

## Question types

### Closed-ended questions
Useful when responses can be represented by predefined categories or scales. They are easier to code and summarize quantitatively.

Examples include:
- yes/no;
- multiple choice;
- Likert-type agreement scales;
- semantic differential scales.

### Open-ended questions
Useful when the researcher needs respondents' own wording, explanations, reasons, or unanticipated categories.

Open-ended data generally require coding, categorization, thematic summary, or direct qualitative interpretation.

## Questionnaire quality checklist

Check that the instrument:
- explains its purpose and gives clear directions;
- uses plain language;
- asks one thing at a time;
- avoids double negatives and ambiguous wording;
- avoids leading questions;
- offers response options that fit the likely answers;
- includes necessary open-ended space where appropriate;
- is not longer than needed;
- respects privacy and practical constraints.

## Interview

### When interviews help
Interviews are useful when the researcher needs depth, explanation, clarification, or follow-up that a fixed questionnaire cannot provide.

### Strengths relative to questionnaires
- allow probing and follow-up;
- can clarify ambiguous responses;
- may produce richer information.

### Limitations
- time intensive;
- typically smaller samples;
- interviewer presence can influence responses;
- transcription and analysis can be demanding;
- some participants may be less comfortable speaking than writing.

### Interview practice
- begin with accessible questions;
- move toward more demanding or personal questions gradually;
- keep wording clear;
- prepare probes, but do not force a preferred answer;
- record or take notes consistently and ethically;
- distinguish participant statements from researcher interpretation.

## Basic descriptive statistics

The book introduces simple descriptive tools for field data. Use them only when appropriate to the data type and research question.

### Central tendency
- mean;
- median;
- mode.

### Dispersion
- range;
- standard deviation.

### Closed-ended responses
Useful summaries include:
- frequency;
- percentage;
- distribution across response categories.

### Open-ended responses
Possible approaches include:
- coding responses into categories and then summarizing counts/percentages;
- direct thematic or qualitative analysis without forcing all responses into numbers.

## Interpretation guardrails

- Descriptive differences do not automatically establish causation.
- A mean can hide distributional differences or outliers.
- Small or biased samples limit generalization.
- Instrument wording affects the meaning of responses.
- Statistical output must be interpreted in relation to the research question, not reported as decoration.

# Research and Topic Selection

## What counts as research

Treat research as a systematic attempt to answer questions or test defensible ideas through careful collection, description, analysis, and interpretation of information. Core characteristics in the book include:
- curiosity or a problem worth investigating;
- explicit questions or hypotheses;
- systematic inquiry;
- careful observation or source use;
- analysis and interpretation;
- conclusions grounded in findings;
- contribution to understanding something previously uncertain or insufficiently explained.

## Major distinctions

### By intention
- **Theoretical research**: seeks to construct, refine, or explain theory and relationships.
- **Applied research**: seeks to address a practical problem, often using or testing theory.

Treat these as a continuum when appropriate rather than mutually exclusive boxes.

### By source of information
- **Primary/field research**: obtains firsthand or original information, such as observation, interviews, questionnaires, experiments, or direct analysis of primary material.
- **Secondary/library research**: uses published or otherwise existing sources.

Strong projects often combine both.

## Requirements for research readiness

Check whether the writer can:
- ask useful questions;
- focus a broad subject into a manageable topic;
- understand the conventional shape of a research paper;
- choose an approach that fits the question;
- document sources consistently.

## Topic-selection criteria

Evaluate candidate topics on four dimensions.

### 1. Interest
The topic should sustain the writer's attention and offer something meaningful to the intended reader. Interest alone is insufficient, but a topic that the writer strongly dislikes is hard to carry through a long project.

### 2. Relevance
The topic should connect to the writer's field, course, program, or legitimate research context. Balance personal interest with disciplinary expectations.

### 3. Significance
Avoid trivial topics. Ask what the study may contribute: clarification, comparison, evidence, application, critique, or a better understanding of a problem.

### 4. Practicality
Check whether the project can actually be completed with available time, sources, access, skills, money, participants, and other resources.

## Five-step topic workflow

### Step 1: Conceptualize the research context
Clarify:
- **reader**: who will read and evaluate the work, and what background can be assumed;
- **purpose**: informative, analytical, argumentative, or another research purpose;
- **time**: total deadline and time allocation across stages;
- **resources**: access to books, articles, participants, instruments, travel, software, or other materials.

### Step 2: Generate multiple topics
Ask the writer to create several lists:
- subjects already known reasonably well;
- subjects worth exploring;
- items in those lists that fit the current project.

Do not commit to the first idea too early.

### Step 3: Conduct preliminary research
Use initial reading or field inquiry to test whether the topic is researchable. Preliminary research should:
- reveal available sources;
- expose key debates or concepts;
- show whether the topic is too broad or too narrow;
- suggest variables, dimensions, or perspectives;
- support a better research question.

### Step 4: Refine the scope
Use dimensions such as:
- time;
- place;
- population or text/corpus;
- aspect or variable;
- specific phenomenon.

**To narrow:** restrict one or more dimensions.

**To broaden:** add a comparative case, extend the time range, expand the population, or connect the issue to a wider analytical dimension.

### Step 5: Formulate a research question
Convert the topic from a label into an answerable question. A useful research question:
- defines what the paper will investigate;
- limits the scope;
- implies what evidence is needed;
- can guide the outline, methods, and discussion.

Avoid a list of loosely related questions. Prefer one central question with clearly subordinate questions when needed.

## Topic-diagnosis prompts

When reviewing a user's topic, ask internally:
- What exactly is the object of study?
- What time/place/population/text is included?
- What relationship, process, difference, interpretation, or effect is being examined?
- Can the user obtain evidence within the deadline?
- Does the topic fit the expected discipline and assignment?
- Is the question answerable without making unsupported causal claims?

## Output standard

When helping select a topic, provide:
1. a sharpened topic title or label;
2. the central research question;
3. scope boundaries;
4. evidence needed;
5. feasibility risks;
6. a preliminary research plan.

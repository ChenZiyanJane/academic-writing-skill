# Research Proposal and Working Outline

## What a proposal must accomplish

Treat a research proposal as both a request for approval and a blueprint for future writing. It should show:
- what the researcher intends to study;
- why the study is worth doing;
- what prior work says;
- how the study will be carried out;
- that the researcher has a feasible plan.

A proposal is provisional. It guides later work but may be revised as research develops.

## Default proposal components

Unless an institution requires a different format, consider:
1. Title
2. Introduction
3. Literature review
4. Methods / methodology
5. Preliminary outline
6. Timetable
7. Bibliography / references

## Title

An effective title should be:
- informative;
- clear about the area or object of study;
- concise enough to read easily;
- specific enough to distinguish the project.

Avoid titles that are clever but uninformative.

## Introduction

The introduction should define the study's boundaries and context. Its common functions are:
- establish the topic;
- provide necessary background;
- identify the problem or issue;
- state the research purpose;
- present research question(s) or hypotheses when appropriate;
- explain significance when needed.

A proposal introduction should move toward the question the study will answer.

## Literature review in a proposal

The literature review should demonstrate that the project is grounded in previous scholarship and that the writer understands the current state of the issue.

It should:
- synthesize prior work;
- identify important concepts or theories;
- show relevant findings and methods;
- evaluate limitations or disagreements;
- lead toward the present research question.

Use `sources-and-literature.md` for organization and synthesis.

## Methods

Describe the design in enough detail for a reader to judge whether it can answer the research question.

Include as relevant:
- participants or data sources;
- sampling or selection;
- instruments or materials;
- procedures;
- data collection;
- planned analysis.

Do not include method detail that has no relationship to the research question.

## Preliminary outline

A proposal should show the likely structure of the eventual paper. The outline is not a contract; it is a test of whether the writer can organize the project logically.

## Timetable

Break the project into stages and assign realistic periods for:
- preliminary research;
- instrument/design preparation;
- data collection;
- analysis;
- drafting;
- revision;
- formatting/submission.

## Bibliography

Treat the bibliography as a working resource list rather than a frozen list. It should evolve as the project develops.

## Purpose of a working outline

A working outline is a logical map of the future paper. It helps:
- keep the writer from getting lost;
- reveal gaps, repetition, and weak sequencing;
- reduce drafting stress;
- improve drafting efficiency;
- make later revision easier.

## Outline formats

### Roman-numeral format
Uses hierarchical labels such as I, A, 1, a.

### Decimal format
Uses hierarchical numbering such as 1.0, 1.1, 1.1.1.

The important issue is not the numbering system but the hierarchy it expresses.

## Outline types

### Topic outline
Uses phrases or short headings. Best for quick, compact planning when the argument is already clear.

### Sentence outline
Uses complete sentences. Best when the writer needs to test exact claims, logical relationships, or a complex argument before drafting.

## Construction workflow

1. State the central research question or thesis.
2. List major ideas needed to answer it.
3. Group related ideas under guiding concepts.
4. Decide major categories or sections.
5. Arrange categories in a logical order.
6. Add subcategories only where they genuinely develop a category.
7. Check hierarchy and balance.
8. Revise as research changes.

## Four outline principles

### Parallelism
Items at the same level should use comparable grammatical form and comparable conceptual scale.

### Coordination
Items at the same level should have roughly equal rank or importance.

### Subordination
Lower-level items should explain, support, specify, divide, or exemplify the item above them.

### Division
When a category is divided, use at least two meaningful parts and apply one consistent basis of division.

## Outline diagnosis

Flag these common failures:
- a section heading merely repeats the paper title;
- a major section is actually a minor example;
- one branch is extremely detailed while a parallel branch is empty without a clear reason;
- headings mix claims, questions, and noun phrases unpredictably;
- a subpoint does not belong under its parent heading;
- a category has only one subcategory;
- the outline does not visibly answer the research question.

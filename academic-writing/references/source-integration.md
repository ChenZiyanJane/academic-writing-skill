# Source Integration, Paraphrase, Documentation, and Plagiarism Prevention

## Integrating outside material

Before inserting source material, decide what function it serves. Outside material should support the writer's reasoning rather than replace it.

Ask:
- Why is this source needed here?
- What point does it support, qualify, challenge, define, or contextualize?
- Is exact wording important, or would paraphrase/summary work better?
- Is the source clearly attributed?
- Does the writer explain the relevance after presenting the source?

## Direct quotation

Use direct quotation when the exact wording is itself important, unusually precise, authoritative, contested, or worth analyzing.

Good practice:
- keep quotations as short as the purpose allows;
- introduce or frame the quotation;
- use quotation marks or block format as required;
- cite the source;
- explain the quotation's significance.

Avoid quote dumping: a quotation should not be expected to make the writer's argument by itself.

## Paraphrase

A paraphrase restates the meaning of a source in the writer's own words and sentence structure while preserving the original idea accurately.

A sound paraphrase should:
- preserve the original meaning;
- change both wording and structure substantially;
- avoid patchwriting or superficial synonym substitution;
- identify the source;
- fit the writer's own argument and prose.

### Paraphrase self-check

After paraphrasing, ask:
1. Is the author/source identified where required?
2. Is the meaning accurate?
3. Is the wording genuinely the writer's own?
4. Is the sentence structure genuinely the writer's own?
5. Has anything important been added, distorted, or omitted?
6. Is the paraphrase cited?

## Summary

Use summary when the writer needs the main idea, pattern, or overall position rather than detailed wording. A summary is more compressed than a paraphrase but still requires attribution when based on a source.

## What counts as plagiarism

Treat as plagiarism or unacceptable source use when a writer:
- presents another person's exact words as their own;
- makes only minor substitutions to copied wording without proper quotation;
- uses another person's ideas without attribution;
- copies distinctive structure or expression so closely that the source is concealed;
- loses track of source boundaries in notes and later presents copied material as original.

## Prevention workflow

1. Record full source information early.
2. Mark exact quotations unmistakably in notes.
3. Keep source language separate from the writer's own commentary.
4. Cite paraphrases and summaries as well as direct quotations.
5. During drafting, attach source information to every borrowed idea.
6. During revision, verify that in-text citations and reference-list entries correspond.

## Notes

Footnotes or endnotes may be useful for:
- citation systems that require them;
- brief supplementary explanation that would interrupt the main text;
- source or textual information not suitable for the main sentence.

Do not use notes to hide essential argumentation that belongs in the body.

## Chinese-language and non-English sources

When a style guide requires transliteration, translation of titles, or bilingual information, follow the current institutional/style requirements. Do not invent transliterations or translations without a reliable basis.

## AI-era integrity extension

Apply the book's source-boundary principles to AI-assisted writing:
- do not fabricate references to make a paragraph appear scholarly;
- do not present unverified generated claims as literature findings;
- preserve the user's actual evidence and citations;
- flag places where a citation is needed rather than inventing one;
- distinguish editing from creating unsupported scholarly content.

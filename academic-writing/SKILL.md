# Academic Writing

## Purpose

Use a research-to-writing workflow rather than treating academic writing as sentence polishing. Help the user move from a researchable question through evidence collection, proposal and outline design, drafting, documentation, revision, and evaluation.

Base the workflow on the nine-unit framework summarized in `references/book-framework.md`. Preserve the book's core distinctions and process logic, but do not treat its 2013-era search interfaces or citation examples as current style-manual authority.

## Core operating rules

1. Start from the user's actual task and available evidence.
2. Separate three layers:
   - research design: what is being asked and how evidence is obtained;
   - argument and paper design: how claims, sections, and evidence are organized;
   - language and presentation: clarity, precision, mechanics, and documentation.
3. Do not repair a weak paper only at the language layer when the research question, evidence, or organization is the real problem.
4. Do not invent sources, citations, quotations, data, participants, methods, results, or literature-review findings.
5. When evidence is missing, identify the gap and explain what evidence would be needed.
6. Distinguish direct quotation, paraphrase, summary, interpretation, and the user's own claim.
7. When rewriting user text, preserve the intended claim unless the user explicitly asks for substantive reframing.
8. Reply in the user's language unless a target-language writing task requires otherwise.
9. For current citation-format details, follow the user's specified style guide or verify the current rules. Treat the book's MLA/APA examples as pedagogical examples, not current canonical formatting.
10. Prefer diagnosis plus action. Tell the user what is wrong, why it matters, and how to fix it.

## Task router

Identify the task before responding.

### A. Topic or idea stage
Read `references/research-topic.md`.

Use when the user has a broad subject, tentative topic, vague interest, or uncertain research question.

Workflow:
1. Check interest, relevance, significance, and practicality.
2. Place the topic in a concrete research context: reader, purpose, time, resources, and access.
3. Narrow or broaden the topic as needed.
4. Run or recommend preliminary research to test researchability.
5. Convert the topic into one or more answerable research questions.

### B. Preliminary research or literature review
Read `references/sources-and-literature.md`.

Use when the user needs sources, source evaluation, literature-review organization, synthesis, research-gap identification, or documentation planning.

Workflow:
1. Clarify what information is needed for the research question.
2. Distinguish primary/field evidence from secondary/library evidence.
3. Evaluate sources for relevance, objectivity, credibility, and fit.
4. Synthesize by guiding concept rather than listing one source after another.
5. Organize chronologically, thematically, or by another explicit principle.
6. Evaluate previous work and show how it leads to the present study.

### C. Field research or basic empirical design
Read `references/field-research.md`.

Use when the user asks about observation, questionnaires, interviews, basic descriptive statistics, or turning field data into reportable findings.

Workflow:
1. Tie the instrument to a research question.
2. Plan who, what, where, when, how, and what will be recorded.
3. Check instrument quality and ethical/common-sense constraints.
4. Separate data collection from data interpretation.
5. Use descriptive statistics only when they answer the research question.

### D. Research proposal
Read `references/proposal-and-outline.md` and `references/sources-and-literature.md`.

Default proposal functions:
- explain what will be studied;
- explain why it matters;
- show what is already known;
- show how the study will be conducted;
- demonstrate feasibility and preparedness.

Check title, introduction, literature review, methodology, preliminary outline, timetable, and bibliography unless the user's institution specifies another structure.

### E. Working outline
Read `references/proposal-and-outline.md`.

Construct or diagnose outlines using:
- hierarchy;
- parallelism;
- coordination;
- subordination;
- division;
- an explicit ordering principle.

Choose a topic outline for compact planning and a sentence outline when logical relationships or complex claims need to be tested.

### F. Drafting a paper or section
Read `references/paper-sections.md`.

For each section, first identify its rhetorical function. Do not judge a section merely by whether it sounds academic.

Typical research-paper functions:
- Abstract: compact account of topic, purpose, background, methods, results, and conclusions/implications as applicable.
- Introduction: introduce topic, review enough prior research to establish the issue, focus the problem, state purpose/questions, and indicate structure when useful.
- Methods: explain what/instruments, who/participants, and how/procedures.
- Results: present and describe findings without turning the section into unsupported interpretation.
- Discussion: interpret findings, connect them to the research purpose and prior work, explain significance, and acknowledge strengths/limitations.
- Conclusion: restate the central outcome, summarize implications, and identify limitations or future research when appropriate.

### G. Quotation, paraphrase, documentation, or plagiarism prevention
Read `references/source-integration.md`.

Before inserting outside material, ask whether it is needed and what function it serves. Prefer paraphrase or summary when exact wording is not itself important. Quote selectively and always attribute the source.

### H. Revision, editing, or whole-paper review
Read `references/revision-and-evaluation.md`.

Use two passes before line editing:
1. content revision;
2. organization revision.

Then edit language, mechanics, consistency, and documentation.

For a whole paper, evaluate section-by-section functions as in Unit 9 rather than giving only general comments.

## Diagnostic order

When a user asks to improve a draft, inspect in this order unless the request is explicitly limited:

1. Research question and purpose
2. Relevance and sufficiency of evidence
3. Claim-evidence relationship
4. Section function
5. Overall organization
6. Paragraph focus and transitions
7. Source integration and documentation
8. Sentence clarity and academic tone
9. Grammar, punctuation, spelling, and formatting

Do not let lower-level language edits hide higher-level research or reasoning problems.

## Evidence discipline

Use these labels internally when analyzing a draft:
- claim: what the writer wants the reader to accept;
- evidence: data, source material, examples, observations, or textual evidence;
- reasoning: the link that explains why the evidence supports the claim;
- qualification: limits, conditions, alternatives, or uncertainty;
- source status: user-provided, retrieved, cited-but-not-provided, or missing.

If a claim has no support, do not manufacture support. Mark it as unsupported or provisional.

## Literature-review discipline

A literature review is not a bibliography in paragraph form. Require it to do at least three things:
1. establish the theoretical or empirical foundation;
2. show what is known, disputed, incomplete, or methodologically limited;
3. lead toward the present research question or purpose.

When several studies are relevant, synthesize across studies before discussing individual detail. Include evaluation, not only summary.

## Rewriting policy

When the user asks for a rewrite:
1. Diagnose the main substantive issue first when useful.
2. Preserve factual meaning and source boundaries.
3. Do not make the prose more certain than the evidence allows.
4. Do not add citations that are not provided or verified.
5. Improve cohesion by making the relation between sentences explicit, not by adding empty transition words.
6. Prefer precise verbs and concrete claims over inflated academic phrasing.

## Default output patterns

Read `references/response-patterns.md` when the task benefits from a structured response.

For draft diagnosis, default to:
- Overall assessment
- Highest-priority problems
- Section or paragraph diagnosis
- Concrete revision plan
- Revised passage, only if requested or clearly useful

For research planning, default to:
- Researchable question
- Scope and key concepts
- Evidence needed
- Proposed method/source plan
- Risks or feasibility issues
- Next writing step

For whole-paper review, default to:
- Research question/purpose
- Structure and section functions
- Evidence and literature use
- Methods/results alignment
- Discussion/conclusion quality
- Documentation and integrity
- Language/editing issues
- Priority revision sequence

## Quality gate

Before finalizing an answer, check:
- Is the research question clear enough to guide the paper?
- Are the claims supported by evidence available to the user?
- Does each major section perform its proper function?
- Is the literature synthesized and evaluated rather than merely listed?
- Are methods aligned with the question?
- Are results separated from interpretation where appropriate?
- Are quotation, paraphrase, and attribution handled transparently?
- Are revision priorities ordered from global to local?
- Have current citation rules been kept distinct from the book's historical examples?
